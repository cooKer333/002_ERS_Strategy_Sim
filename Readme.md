# ERS Physics: Power vs. Lap Time

This document explains the core physics behind the ERS (Energy Recovery System) simulator, specifically how adding power translates into time saved on a lap.

---

## 1. The Physical Model

In Formula 1, most engine power at high speeds is spent overcoming **Drag** (air resistance). When we deploy ERS, we aren't just adding speed; we are adding a "boost" force that helps the car cut through the air more efficiently.

### The Variables

| Variable | Description | Simple Analogy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **$P_{ers}$** | Additional ERS power (Watts) | The strength of your "boost" button. |
| **$F_{baseline}$** | Resistance force at current speed | The "wind" pushing against the car. |
| **$v_{avg}$** | Baseline speed in the zone | How fast you were already going. |
| **$\Delta t$** | Time saved | The "Delta" (negative means faster). |

---

## 2. The Formula

To estimate the time saving, we use a linear approximation of the power-to-force relationship:

$$\Delta t \approx -\frac{P_{ers} \times \text{duration}}{F_{baseline} \times v_{avg}}$$

To derive the formula $\Delta t \approx -\frac{P_{ers} \times \text{duration}}{F_{baseline} \times v_{avg}}$, we look at how a small change in force affects the time it takes to cover a set distance.

#### 1. The Relationship Between Power and Force

First, we define the extra force ($F_{ers}$) generated by the ERS. Since Power ($P$) is Force ($F$) multiplied by Velocity ($v$):


$$P = F \times v \implies F_{ers} = \frac{P_{ers}}{v}$$

#### 2. Time to Cover a Distance

The time ($t$) required to cover a fixed distance ($d$) at a constant velocity ($v$) is:


$$t = \frac{d}{v}$$


In a racing context, velocity is maintained by the force of the engine overcoming resistance (drag). If we add $F_{ers}$, the car accelerates, but for a "layman" or "linear" approximation, we treat it as an increase in the steady-state velocity over that distance.

#### 3. Comparing the Two Scenarios

* **Baseline Time ($t_1$):** The time taken using only the baseline force ($F_b$).
* **Boosted Time ($t_2$):** The time taken when $F_{ers}$ is added.

The change in time is:


$$\Delta t = t_2 - t_1$$

Using the principle that velocity is proportional to force in a resistance-limited environment (where $F = \text{ma}$ and we are looking at the delta in "push"), we can express the time saved by looking at the ratio of the forces:


$$\frac{t_2}{t_1} = \frac{F_{baseline}}{F_{baseline} + F_{ers}}$$

#### 4. The Linear Approximation

If the extra force $F_{ers}$ is small compared to the baseline force, we can use a **Taylor Series expansion** (specifically $1/(1+x) \approx 1-x$).

$$t_2 = t_1 \left( \frac{1}{1 + \frac{F_{ers}}{F_{baseline}}} \right) \approx t_1 \left( 1 - \frac{F_{ers}}{F_{baseline}} \right)$$

Now, calculate $\Delta t$:


$$\Delta t = t_2 - t_1 \approx t_1 \left( 1 - \frac{F_{ers}}{F_{baseline}} \right) - t_1$$

$$\Delta t \approx -t_1 \times \frac{F_{ers}}{F_{baseline}}$$

#### 5. Final Substitution

Substitute the definitions from Step 1 and Step 2 back into the equation:

* Replace $t_1$ with **duration** (the time the boost is active).
* Replace $F_{ers}$ with $\frac{P_{ers}}{v_{avg}}$.

$$\Delta t \approx -\text{duration} \times \frac{\frac{P_{ers}}{v_{avg}}}{F_{baseline}}$$

Rearranging this gives the final project formula:


$$\Delta t \approx -\frac{P_{ers} \times \text{duration}}{F_{baseline} \times v_{avg}}$$

---

**What this tells us physics-wise:**
The time saved is directly proportional to the total **Energy** ($P \times \text{duration}$) added, but it is inversely proportional to how fast you are already going and how much resistance you are already fighting. This is why ERS "feels" more powerful at the start of a straight than at the very end.

### Why this works:

1. **Extra Force:** $F_{ers} = \frac{P}{v}$. Power divided by speed gives us the extra "pushing" force.
2. **Time Delta:** By adding this force to the car's existing baseline force, the car covers the same distance in a shorter duration.
3. **The Negative Sign:** In racing, a "negative" delta is good—it means you subtracted time from your lap.

## 3. Real-World Benchmarks

According to real-world F1 telemetry analysis, the relationship between energy (Megajoules) and time is roughly:

> **Rule of Thumb:** Every **2 MJ** of energy deployed per lap equals approximately **+0.3s to +0.5s** of lap time gain.

---

## 4. Implementation Notes for Simulator

* **High Speed vs. Low Speed:** ERS is more effective at lower speeds because $F = \frac{P}{v}$. As $v$ (speed) increases, the same amount of power ($P$) produces less force ($F$).
* **Efficiency:** `DEPLOY_EFFICIENCY` factor (usually ~95%) to the raw battery power to accounts for heat and mechanical losses.
